# Comprehensive chess curriculum: Beginner to master

**Version:** 1.1  
**Updated:** July 12, 2026  
**Audience:** Complete beginners through experienced competitive players who
want a systematic path to master-level competence  
**Primary format:** Classical over-the-board chess, supported by rapid games,
calculation exercises, annotated master games, and deliberate endgame practice

## Purpose

This curriculum develops the complete competitive player. It is not a list of
videos to watch or openings to memorize. Every unit combines:

1. **Knowledge:** The player can explain the concept.
2. **Recognition:** The player identifies it in unfamiliar positions.
3. **Calculation:** The player proves a move without moving the pieces.
4. **Execution:** The player applies it in serious games.
5. **Review:** The player diagnoses errors and updates future training.

The curriculum follows a spiral design. Important ideas recur at increasing
depth. For example, an intermediate learns opposition, an advanced player
calculates corresponding squares, and a master candidate studies reciprocal
zugzwang and exceptions in practical pawn endings.

The document uses simple, portable Markdown, sentence-case headings, active
voice, and descriptive links in line with the
[Google Markdown style guide](https://google.github.io/styleguide/docguide/style.html)
and the broader
[Google documentation style guide](https://developers.google.com/style/highlights).

## Scope and rating bands

Ratings from different pools are not interchangeable. Use the bands as rough
placement guides and advance by demonstrated mastery, not rating alone.

| Stage | Approximate OTB strength | Main outcome |
|---|---:|---|
| Stage A: Absolute beginner | Unrated-600 | Learn the board, rules, notation, and how games end |
| Stage B: Novice player | 600-900 | Stop hanging pieces, spot basic tactics, and finish legal games |
| Stage C: Developing player | 900-1200 | Apply a thinking process, opening principles, and basic endgame technique |
| Stage 1: Intermediate foundations | 1200-1500 | Stop routine tactical losses and play principled complete games |
| Stage 2: Strong intermediate | 1500-1800 | Calculate forcing lines, understand structures, and convert basic endings |
| Stage 3: Advanced club player | 1800-2000 | Build a coherent repertoire and make position-specific strategic decisions |
| Stage 4: Expert and candidate master | 2000-2200 | Prepare deeply, defend accurately, and perform consistently in strong events |
| Stage 5: Master preparation | 2200-2400+ | Produce master-level decisions, preparation, technique, and tournament performance |

FIDE title requirements are separate from curriculum completion. Under the
current FIDE title regulations, the usual rating route is 2200 for Candidate
Master and 2300 for FIDE Master. International Master and Grandmaster titles
normally also require norms and higher rating thresholds. Consult the
[current FIDE title regulations](https://handbook.fide.com/chapter/B012024)
before planning a title campaign.

## Starting point

Stage A has no chess prerequisites. A learner needs only a board or digital
board, a way to play legal games, and willingness to practice regularly.

The following skills are the readiness standard for entering Stage 1. Stages
A-C teach all of them:

- Set up the board and make every legal move, including castling, en passant,
  and promotion.
- Recognize check, checkmate, stalemate, insufficient material, and the basic
  draw rules.
- Read and write algebraic notation.
- Mate with king and queen against king and king and rook against king.
- Explain development, center control, king safety, and material value.
- Play a complete 30-minute game without illegal moves.

FIDE recognizes algebraic notation for its competitions. See
[FIDE Appendix C](https://rcc.fide.com/appendixc/) and the
[FIDE Laws of Chess](https://rcc.fide.com/fide-laws-of-chess_fulltexthtml/)
for the official rules.

## How to use the curriculum

### Recommended training cycle

Use a four-week cycle within every stage:

1. **Acquire:** Learn the idea from explanations and model positions.
2. **Retrieve:** Solve positions from memory and explain the solution.
3. **Apply:** Play training and tournament games chosen to expose the theme.
4. **Test and repair:** Take a mixed test, analyze mistakes, and repeat weak
   skills.

Do not advance because the calendar says to. Advance when the exit standards
are met twice, at least one week apart.

### Weekly workload

| Stage | Hours per week | Serious games per month | Suggested study split |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| A | 2-3 | 4 guided games | 35% rules/board vision, 25% play, 20% mates, 20% review |
| B | 3-5 | 6-8 | 40% tactics, 25% play, 15% mates/endgames, 10% openings, 10% review |
| C | 4-6 | 6-8 | 40% tactics, 20% play, 15% endgames, 10% openings, 15% analysis |
| 1 | 6-8 | 4 | 35% tactics, 20% endgames, 15% openings, 15% game analysis, 15% strategy |
| 2 | 8-10 | 4-6 | 30% tactics/calculation, 20% endgames, 15% openings, 20% analysis, 15% strategy |
| 3 | 10-14 | 6 | 25% calculation, 20% endgames, 20% openings, 20% analysis, 15% strategy |
| 4 | 14-18 | 6-8 | 25% calculation, 20% openings, 20% endgames, 25% analysis/preparation, 10% physical/mental work |
| 5 | 18-25 | 8-12 | Individualized from error statistics and event schedule |

At Stages A and B, use enough time to identify every legal move and check every
capture. From Stage C onward, at least half of playing time should use a slow
control. A useful minimum is 30 minutes plus increment; classical tournament
games are preferable.

### Standard study session

1. Warm up with 5-10 easy pattern problems.
2. Perform one demanding block of 30-60 minutes without distractions.
3. Record complete variations and evaluations before checking an answer.
4. Compare with an annotated solution, then an engine only after independent
   analysis.
5. Write one concise lesson and schedule failed positions for repetition.

## Initial diagnostic

Complete this diagnostic without an engine. Use the results to enter at the
lowest stage where two or more areas fail.

### Beginner placement screen

| Skill | If not yet reliable, begin at |
|---|---|
| Set up the board with a light square at each player's right | Stage A |
| Move every piece legally and identify check | Stage A |
| Explain checkmate, stalemate, castling, promotion, and en passant | Stage A |
| Record a short game in algebraic notation | Stage B |
| Mate with king and queen and with king and rook | Stage B |
| Avoid leaving a piece en prise for one move in most games | Stage C |
| Recognize fork, pin, skewer, discovered attack, and back-rank mate | Stage C |
| Use opening principles and finish development before launching attacks | Stage C |
| Demonstrate opposition, key squares, and the rule of the square | Stage C |

If the learner cannot yet play a complete legal game, begin at Stage A
regardless of online rating. If all beginner skills are reliable, continue with
the full diagnostic below.

| Domain | Diagnostic | Passing evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Tactical vision | 30 mixed problems at appropriate difficulty | At least 80% correct with no single-move blunders |
| Calculation | 6 positions, 10-15 minutes each | Legal candidate lines, stable board visualization, justified final evaluation |
| Endgames | 20 essential positions | At least 16 correct against a sparring partner or tablebase |
| Openings | Explain plans in 10 repertoire positions | Names are optional; pawn breaks, bad pieces, and typical endings are correct |
| Strategy | Annotate 3 quiet positions | At least two sensible candidate moves and a position-based plan in each |
| Game analysis | Analyze one recent loss | Critical moments, alternatives, and root causes identified before engine use |
| Practical play | Review 10 slow games | Fewer than two unforced one-move losses and consistent clock use |

Create an error log with these tags:

- `TAC`: missed tactic or tactical blunder
- `CALC`: calculation or visualization failure
- `STRAT`: evaluation or planning failure
- `OPEN`: opening knowledge or move-order failure
- `END`: endgame knowledge or technique failure
- `TIME`: poor clock allocation
- `PSYCH`: emotion, confidence, focus, or risk-management failure
- `RULE`: notation, claim, or tournament-procedure failure

Track both frequency and cost. A recurring half-point loss deserves more
attention than an exotic position that has never occurred.

# Part 0: Beginner foundations

## Board, pieces, and coordinates

The learner should master the physical and visual language of chess before
studying openings.

- Orient the board with a light square on each player's right.
- Place queens on their own colors and identify kingside and queenside.
- Name every file, rank, square, diagonal, wing, and center square.
- Identify White's and Black's perspective without rotating the notation.
- Move rooks, bishops, queens, knights, kings, and pawns legally.
- Understand that pieces cannot jump except for knights and castling.
- Distinguish an occupied square, attacked square, defended piece, and legal
  destination.
- Count material using the rough beginner scale: pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3,
  rook 5, and queen 9. Treat king safety and activity as more important than
  blindly following these values.

### Coordinate drills

- Point to a called square within three seconds.
- Name the color of a square without touching the board.
- Trace files, ranks, bishop diagonals, and knight routes.
- Set up a position from a simple diagram or FEN with supervision.
- Say a move aloud and then write it in algebraic notation.

## Complete rules syllabus

### Movement and capture

- Pawns move forward and capture diagonally.
- A pawn may advance two squares only from its starting rank when unobstructed.
- En passant is available only immediately after the qualifying two-square pawn
  advance.
- Promotion is mandatory on the final rank and may create a queen, rook,
  bishop, or knight.
- A king may not move into check or remain in check.
- A pinned piece may still attack a square even when it cannot legally move
  there without exposing its king.

### Check and legal responses

When in check, the player must use one of the legal responses:

1. Move the king to a safe square.
2. Capture the checking piece.
3. Block the checking line when the check comes from a sliding piece and a
   blocking square exists.

Double check can be answered only by moving the king. Knight, pawn, and contact
checks cannot be blocked.

### Castling

- Castle kingside and queenside with the king moving two squares toward the
  rook and the rook crossing to the adjacent square.
- Castling is illegal if the king or relevant rook has previously moved.
- Castling is illegal when pieces remain between king and rook.
- The king may not castle while in check, through check, or into check.
- The rook may begin attacked, and the queen's rook may cross an attacked
  square, provided every condition on the king is satisfied.

### Ways a game ends

- Checkmate.
- Resignation.
- Draw by stalemate.
- Draw by agreement.
- Draw by repetition under the applicable claiming or automatic rule.
- Draw under the 50-move or 75-move provisions.
- Draw when no legal sequence can lead to checkmate.
- Loss on time, subject to the opponent having a possible mating sequence.
- Event-specific outcomes involving illegal moves or forfeiture.

Beginners should learn the concept first, then consult the current
[FIDE Laws of Chess](https://rcc.fide.com/fide-laws-of-chess_fulltexthtml/)
for exact competition procedures.

## Beginner notation syllabus

- Piece letters: K, Q, R, B, and N; pawns use no piece letter.
- Destination square for every move.
- `x` for capture.
- `+` for check and `#` for checkmate.
- `O-O` and `O-O-O` for castling.
- Promotion notation such as `e8=Q` or `fxg8=N+`.
- File or rank disambiguation when identical pieces can reach the same square.
- Result notation: `1-0`, `0-1`, and `1/2-1/2`.
- Move numbers and ellipses for Black-to-move fragments.

The learner should record at least five short games by hand or from memory and
correct notation errors immediately after each game.

## Beginner checkmate syllabus

### One-move mates

- Queen or rook protected by the king.
- Back-rank mate.
- Ladder mate with two rooks or rook and queen.
- Queen beside the king when safely protected.
- Basic mating contact squares and escape-square control.

### Fundamental mating procedures

- King and queen versus king: restrict the king, bring up the king, then mate
  without stalemating.
- King and rook versus king: build a shrinking box, use king opposition, and
  deliver mate on the edge.
- Two rooks versus king: use the staircase method and protect against captures.
- Queen and king versus a pawn near promotion: approach with checks while
  recognizing rook-pawn and bishop-pawn stalemate exceptions.

### Mate-in-two thinking

- Identify every legal check.
- Find all opponent king escapes, captures, and blocks.
- Choose the move that answers every defense.
- Recognize when a quiet move creates an unavoidable mate.

## Beginner material and safety syllabus

- Count attackers and defenders before a capture.
- Identify pieces that are undefended or attacked more times than defended.
- Apply the least-valuable-attacker idea carefully.
- Understand equal trades and favorable trades.
- Do not trade a high-value piece for a lower-value piece without compensation.
- Scan the entire board after every opponent move.
- Ask, "What does that move attack?"
- Before moving, ask, "Can my opponent capture the piece or mate me?"
- Learn that winning a queen does not matter if the king is checkmated.

## Beginner opening syllabus

Beginners need principles and recurring positions, not deep memorization.

### Opening goals

1. Influence the center with pawns and pieces.
2. Develop knights and bishops efficiently.
3. Castle before the king becomes exposed.
4. Avoid moving the same piece repeatedly without a concrete reason.
5. Avoid bringing the queen out where it can be chased.
6. Connect the rooks and choose a middlegame plan.

### Starter opening positions

Teach one simple, principled path rather than an inflexible system:

- As White after 1.e4: learn positions from the Italian Game, then basic
  responses to the Sicilian, French, and Caro-Kann.
- As Black against 1.e4: begin with 1...e5 and develop naturally against the
  Italian, Ruy Lopez, Scotch, and early gambits.
- As Black against 1.d4: begin with 1...d5 and use Queen's Gambit Declined
  development against c4 systems.
- Against unusual first moves: occupy or influence the center, develop, and
  castle instead of trying to refute the opening immediately.

### Opening mistakes to recognize

- Scholar's Mate attack and defense.
- Fool's Mate and weakened king diagonals.
- Early queen raids and how to gain tempi without chasing blindly.
- Greedy pawn captures that neglect development.
- Moving the f-pawn and exposing the king.
- Developing pieces to squares where pawns can trap them.
- Failing to answer a center threat.
- Copying the opponent's moves without checking tactics.

## Beginner tactical syllabus

Introduce motifs in this order:

1. Hanging piece and free capture.
2. Mate in one and mate in two.
3. Pawn, knight, bishop, rook, queen, and king forks.
4. Absolute and relative pins.
5. Skewers.
6. Discovered attacks and discovered checks.
7. Double attacks.
8. Removal of a defender.
9. Back-rank tactics and creating luft.
10. Simple deflection, attraction, and promotion tactics.

Every exercise should require the learner to name the opponent's threat before
searching for their own tactic. Include positions where the correct move is
defensive so that "puzzle mode" does not replace real chess thinking.

## Beginner endgame syllabus

- Activate the king when queens and most pieces are gone.
- Promote passed pawns and stop opposing passed pawns.
- Use the rule of the square.
- Learn direct opposition.
- Learn key squares for king and pawn versus king.
- Win with a protected passed pawn.
- Create a passed pawn by majority and breakthrough.
- Recognize rook-pawn stalemate patterns.
- Place rooks behind passed pawns as a useful default, with later exceptions.
- Avoid unnecessary checks that drive the enemy king toward the desired square.
- Convert a large material advantage by trading pieces, not automatically all
  pawns.

## Beginner game-analysis routine

After every slow or guided game, answer only these questions at first:

1. Where did the first material loss occur?
2. Was either king exposed unnecessarily?
3. Which opening goal was completed last?
4. Was a basic tactic missed by either side?
5. If the game reached an ending, was the king activated?
6. What single habit should change in the next game?

Do not overwhelm a new player with engine evaluations. First correct illegal
moves, missed checks, free pieces, and basic mates.

# Part I: Core competency map

## Tactical pattern syllabus

The goal is not merely to know the names. For every pattern, learn the trigger,
mechanism, calculation demands, defensive resources, and common failure mode.

### Forcing-move foundations

- Checks: contact, protected, distant, discovered, double, cross-check, and
  checking sacrifice.
- Captures: favorable exchanges, intermediate captures, removal of defenders,
  and capture sequences that change move order.
- Threats: mate threats, promotion threats, double attacks, trapped-piece
  threats, and threats that force concessions.
- Candidate-move order: checks, forcing captures, direct threats, opponent's
  threats, then quiet improvements.

### Attack relationships

- Fork and double attack by queen, rook, bishop, knight, king, or pawn.
- Pin: absolute, relative, situational, cross-pin, and breaking or exploiting a
  pin.
- Skewer and reverse skewer.
- Discovered attack, discovered check, and double check.
- X-ray attack and x-ray defense.
- Battery, alignment, and line-piece geometry.
- Overloading and multiple defensive duties.
- Loose pieces, hanging pieces, and inadequately defended pieces.
- Trapped or dominated pieces.

### Defender manipulation

- Removal of the defender.
- Deflection and decoy.
- Attraction to a square, file, rank, or diagonal.
- Interference and line blocking.
- Clearance of a square, line, diagonal, or file.
- Blocking a flight square or defensive line.
- Annihilation of pawn cover.
- Undermining and destruction of a pawn chain.
- Exchange sacrifice to remove a key defender.

### Move-order tactics

- Zwischenzug, intermediate check, and intermediate capture.
- Desperado and maximizing value from a doomed piece.
- Tempo attacks and gaining time with threats.
- Countercheck and perpetual-check mechanisms.
- Transposition tricks and move-order traps.
- Quiet moves in forcing combinations.

### Mating patterns

- Back-rank mate and back-rank weakness.
- Corridor and box mates.
- Smothered mate and semi-smothered mate.
- Anastasia's mate.
- Arabian mate.
- Boden's mate.
- Blackburne's mate.
- Damiano's mate.
- Legal's mate and related queen-sacrifice patterns.
- Lolli and Greco mating patterns.
- Morphy mate.
- Opera mate.
- Pillsbury mate.
- Epaulette mate.
- Hook mate.
- Dovetail and swallowtail mates.
- Double-bishop mate.
- Bishop-and-rook battery mates.
- Queen-and-knight mating nets.
- Rook lift and rook swing attacks.
- h-file and g-file mating patterns.
- Greek Gift sacrifice on h7 or h2, including when it fails.
- Sacrifices on f7, f2, g7, g2, h6, and h3.
- Castled-king attacks with opposite-side and same-side castling.
- King hunts and mating-net construction.

### Pawn and promotion tactics

- Pawn breakthrough.
- Protected, connected, outside, remote, and passed-pawn tactics.
- Promotion with tempo, checking promotion, and promotion forks.
- Underpromotion to knight, rook, or bishop.
- Stalemate by promotion choice.
- En passant as a tactical resource, including opening a file.
- Promotion races and counting tempos.
- Deflection by a passed pawn.

### Defensive tactics

- Counterattack and counter-threat.
- Tactical simplification and queen exchange.
- Perpetual check.
- Stalemate combination.
- Fortress construction.
- Sacrificial destruction of the attack.
- Returning material to extinguish threats.
- Interposition, flight-square creation, and luft.
- Defensive pin, skewer, and zwischenzug.
- Drawing combinations through repetition or insufficient material.
- Prophylactic tactics: preventing the opponent's forcing idea before it starts.

### Advanced combination themes

- Double and triple sacrifices.
- Geometric combinations involving several lines.
- Domination and restricted mobility.
- Positional sacrifice for squares, color control, initiative, or blockade.
- Exchange sacrifice for structure, minor-piece strength, or king exposure.
- Clearance sacrifices and interference sacrifices.
- Windmill and seesaw.
- Mill attacks on king and piece.
- Tactical transformation into a winning endgame.
- Tactical draws in lost positions.
- Composed-study ideas: Novotny interference, Plachutta interference,
  Grimshaw interference, systematic maneuvers, and reciprocal zugzwang.

## Calculation and visualization syllabus

### Candidate generation

- Identify the opponent's last-move changes.
- Run a blunder check for checks, captures, and threats for both sides.
- Generate forcing moves first without assuming they are best.
- Add positional candidates, defensive candidates, and simplifying candidates.
- Limit the candidate set by comparison, not by intuition alone.

### Tree management

- Calculate one branch to a stable evaluation before switching.
- Use move-order discipline and avoid blending branches.
- Identify forcing nodes, choice nodes, and evaluation nodes.
- Prune only after refuting a line or proving another candidate superior.
- Stop at quiescent positions, not merely after a fixed number of moves.
- Compare final positions using material, king safety, activity, structure,
  space, and concrete tactics.

### Visualization

- Square-color and coordinate drills.
- Knight-route and bishop-diagonal exercises.
- Track removed and relocated pieces through exchanges.
- Reconstruct a position after a spoken variation.
- Solve progressively longer lines without moving pieces.
- Practice blindfold fragments, then full games only when accurate.

### Calculation types

- Forcing combinations.
- Quiet tactical positions.
- Strategic calculation with several plausible moves.
- Endgame calculation with exact tempo counting.
- Defensive calculation and only-move searches.
- Comparison exercises: choose between two reasonable moves.
- Evaluation exercises: receive a final position and assess it without finding
  the preceding moves.
- Decision exercises with a clock and no hint that a tactic exists.

### Calculation protocol

Use the following protocol until it becomes automatic:

1. State the position's tactical facts and the opponent's threat.
2. List two to four candidates.
3. Calculate the most forcing candidate first.
4. At each opponent turn, find their strongest reply, not the most natural one.
5. Stop at a stable position and evaluate it.
6. Compare candidates.
7. Before committing, perform a final legality and blunder scan.

## Strategic and positional syllabus

### Evaluation framework

- Material quantity and material quality.
- King safety and king activity.
- Development and initiative.
- Piece activity, coordination, mobility, and stability.
- Pawn structure and pawn breaks.
- Space and territorial control.
- Weak squares, color complexes, and entry points.
- Open files, half-open files, diagonals, ranks, and outposts.
- Permanent versus temporary advantages.
- Static versus dynamic positions.
- Best and worst pieces for both sides.
- Favorable and unfavorable exchanges.
- Transformation from one advantage to another.

### Pawn structures

For every structure, learn typical piece placement, pawn breaks, exchanges,
king-safety patterns, favorable endings, and model games.

- Isolated queen's pawn: blockade, activity, e5/c5 breaks, and liquidation.
- Hanging pawns: dynamic advance, restraint, and creation of targets.
- Carlsbad structure: minority attack, central break, kingside attack, and
  opposite-side plans.
- Orthodox queen's gambit structures.
- Slav and Semi-Slav structures.
- Panov structure.
- French chains: base attack, head attack, c5 and f6 breaks.
- Caro-Kann structures and minority endgames.
- Open Sicilian structures: backward d6 pawn, d5 square, queenside majority,
  and opposite-side attacks.
- Maroczy Bind: space, restraint, breaks with b5 and d5.
- Scheveningen, Najdorf, Dragon, and Sveshnikov structures.
- King's Indian locked center: c5 and f5 attacks, timing, and closed-center
  king safety.
- Benoni structure: queenside majority versus kingside space.
- Benko structure: long-term queenside files and exchange sacrifices.
- Stonewall: e5 outpost, bad bishop, and kingside attack.
- Hedgehog: elastic defense and b5/d5 breaks.
- English Botvinnik structure.
- Symmetrical English and reversed Sicilian structures.
- Open center, mobile center, closed center, fixed center, and fluid center.
- Doubled, isolated, backward, connected, and passed pawns.

### Piece play

- Knight outposts, blockades, rim-knight exceptions, and domination.
- Good and bad bishops, active bad bishops, and bishop outside the pawn chain.
- Bishop pair in open and semi-open positions.
- Opposite-colored bishops in attacks and endings.
- Rook activity, open files, seventh rank, doubling, lifts, and lateral defense.
- Queen centralization, queen safety, and coordination with minor pieces.
- King safety in the middlegame and king centralization in the endgame.
- Exchanges: trade the right piece, not automatically equal-value pieces.

### Planning and prophylaxis

- Identify the opponent's threat and desired pawn break.
- Improve the worst-placed piece.
- Create or attack a second weakness.
- Restrict counterplay before converting.
- Choose the correct wing based on the pawn chain and king locations.
- Accumulate small advantages without allowing tactical release.
- Decide when to open or close the position.
- Convert space into a breakthrough.
- Use prophylactic moves that preserve flexibility.
- Switch plans when the position's defining feature changes.

### Dynamic play

- Initiative and development lead.
- Lead in mobilization as compensation for material.
- Pawn storms and opposite-side castling.
- Central breaks against flank attacks.
- Sacrifices for time, lines, squares, or king exposure.
- Practical compensation and long-term compensation.
- Attack-to-endgame transitions.
- Defense by activity rather than passivity.

## Essential techniques

### Thinking and decision-making

- Use a consistent move-selection routine.
- Distinguish tactics, calculation, and evaluation.
- Know when general principles have concrete exceptions.
- Make decisions under uncertainty without inventing certainty.
- Match risk to tournament situation, position, and opponent.
- Avoid automatic recaptures and automatic checks.
- Reassess after every pawn move, exchange, and change in king safety.

### Conversion

- Simplify when the resulting position is demonstrably favorable.
- Keep enough winning material and avoid accidental fortresses.
- Restrict counterplay before collecting material.
- Create a second weakness.
- Improve the king and worst piece.
- Convert one advantage into another: initiative to material, space to a pawn
  break, structure to an endgame, or attack to perpetual safety.
- Use the principle of two weaknesses without forcing it mechanically.

### Defense

- Identify the opponent's actual threat.
- Exchange attacking pieces and preserve key defenders.
- Close lines, return material, or seek counterplay.
- Choose active, passive, or tactical defense based on concrete demands.
- Build a fortress and test every entry route.
- Seek stalemate, perpetual check, opposite-colored bishops, or rook activity.
- Make the opponent solve new problems instead of resigning psychologically.

### Clock management

- Establish time checkpoints before the game.
- Spend time at irreversible decisions and critical changes.
- Avoid using large amounts of time on familiar forced recaptures.
- Reserve time for the final phase and increment technique.
- In time trouble, favor legal forcing moves, stable king safety, and simple
  positions, but do not simplify into a lost ending.
- Record recurring time-use errors in the error log.

### Game analysis

Analyze every serious game in this order:

1. Reconstruct thoughts, candidates, evaluations, and clock times.
2. Mark critical moments without an engine.
3. Analyze alternatives with a board and, when possible, a coach or peer.
4. Consult opening references and annotated master games.
5. Use an engine to test tactics and evaluations.
6. Use a tablebase for eligible endgames.
7. Write no more than three actionable lessons.
8. Add missed patterns to spaced repetition and repair the repertoire file.

## Endgame theory syllabus

### Fundamental checkmates

- King and queen against king.
- King and rook against king using box and opposition methods.
- Two rooks against king.
- Two bishops against king.
- Bishop and knight against king, including the W maneuver.
- Queen or rook against advanced pawns.
- Recognize drawn material: lone minor piece, two knights without a cooperative
  pawn, and other insufficient-material cases under the rules.

### Pawn endings

- Opposition: direct, distant, diagonal, and corresponding opposition.
- Key squares and critical squares.
- Rule of the square and exceptions.
- Shouldering and outflanking.
- Reserve tempi and spare pawn moves.
- Zugzwang, mutual zugzwang, and reciprocal zugzwang.
- Corresponding squares.
- Protected, connected, outside, and remote passed pawns.
- Candidate passed pawns and breakthroughs.
- Pawn races, promotion with check, and stalemate motifs.
- Triangulation.
- Trebuchet.
- Rook-pawn exceptions.
- Queen-versus-pawn promotion races on different files.
- Multi-pawn endings: fixing weaknesses, creating passers, and king routes.

### Rook endings

- Rook activity before pawn grabbing.
- Rook behind a passed pawn, with known exceptions.
- Cutting off the king.
- Lucena position and building a bridge.
- Philidor position and third-rank defense.
- Short-side defense and checking distance.
- Long-side and side-check defenses.
- Vancura defense.
- Frontal defense.
- Rook and pawn versus rook with rook, knight, bishop, and central pawns.
- Rook pawn plus rook versus rook.
- Two connected pawns versus rook.
- Rook versus separated pawns.
- Active rook versus passive rook.
- Rook and four versus rook and three on one wing.
- Three versus three and four versus four practical endings.
- Rook and minor piece versus rook.
- Rook versus minor piece and pawns.
- Double-rook endings and transition to single-rook endings.
- Checking from the side, rear, and front.
- King shelter from checks.
- Pawn-race calculation and tactical rook sacrifices.

### Bishop endings

- Same-colored bishops: king activity, opposition, and fixed pawns.
- Opposite-colored bishops as drawing resource.
- Opposite-colored bishops with rooks or queens as attacking positions.
- Good bishop versus bad bishop.
- Wrong-colored bishop and rook pawn.
- Bishop outside the pawn chain.
- Bishop versus pawns on both wings.
- Fortresses and sacrifice for the final pawn.

### Knight endings

- Knight and pawn versus knight.
- Knight blockades and stable outposts.
- Knight's difficulty stopping rook pawns.
- Knight endings as pawn endings, with important tactical exceptions.
- Domination, forks, and tempo maneuvers.
- Knight versus passed pawns on one or both wings.

### Bishop-versus-knight endings

- Open versus closed position.
- Pawns on one wing versus both wings.
- Fixed color weaknesses.
- Stable knight outposts and bishop targets.
- Creating a favorable pawn structure before exchanging.
- Good minor piece versus bad minor piece.

### Queen endings

- Perpetual-check geometry and king shelters.
- Queen centralization and checking distance.
- Queen and pawn versus queen.
- Queen versus advanced pawn.
- Queen exchange calculation.
- Cross-checks, skewers, and promotion threats.
- Fortress constructions and stalemate resources.
- Transition from queen ending to pawn ending.

### Mixed-material and advanced endings

- Queen versus rook with and without pawns.
- Queen versus minor pieces.
- Rook and bishop versus rook, including Philidor, Cochrane, second-rank, and
  defensive fortress ideas.
- Rook and knight versus rook.
- Exchange-up endings and fortress risk.
- Rook versus bishop or knight with pawns.
- Two minor pieces versus rook.
- Queen and rook coordination.
- Opposite-colored bishops with major pieces.
- Fortresses, domination, stalemate, and perpetual attack.
- Endgame transitions: whether to trade queens, rooks, or the final minor piece.

The
[Lichess practice collection](https://lichess.org/practice)
includes key pawn and rook positions such as opposition, Lucena, and Philidor.
Use legal-position tablebases to verify exact endgame claims, but study the
human method needed to reproduce the result over the board.

# Part II: Opening curriculum

## Opening study principles

A strong repertoire is narrow enough to remember and broad enough to survive
sidelines. It should contain positions you understand, not a catalog of opening
names.

Study each opening in this order:

1. Typical pawn structure and strategic objective.
2. Development scheme and king placement.
3. Thematic pawn breaks.
4. Best and worst pieces.
5. Standard tactical motifs.
6. Common move-order traps and early deviations.
7. Favorable and unfavorable exchanges.
8. Typical endgames.
9. Five to ten model games.
10. Only then, concrete main-line theory.

Use the
[Lichess opening explorer](https://lichess.org/opening)
to investigate move trees and filter by speed and rating. Statistics describe
what has been played; they do not by themselves prove that a move is best.

## Repertoire construction standard

Every player should maintain one living repertoire file with:

- One main White first move and a coherent response to every major defense.
- One main Black defense to 1.e4.
- One main Black system against 1.d4, 1.c4, and 1.Nf3, with move-order notes.
- A surprise or lower-theory alternative only after the main repertoire is
  stable.
- A position card at each important branch containing evaluation, plan, pawn
  break, tactical warning, model game, and next review date.

Depth targets:

| Stage | Expected preparation depth |
|---|---|
| 1 | Principles and first 6-8 moves; recognize traps |
| 2 | Main branches to 10-12 moves; know plans and model games |
| 3 | Critical tabiyas to 12-18 moves; maintain personal notes |
| 4 | Opponent-specific branches, novelties, and deeply analyzed tabiyas |
| 5 | Continually updated files, independent analysis, and event preparation |

Move count is a rough guide, not a goal. A forced line may require 25 moves;
a quiet structure may require only 8 moves and strong understanding.

## White repertoire families

Choose one primary family. The alternatives are included for literacy and
future repertoire growth.

### 1.e4

Core responses to prepare:

- **1...e5:** Italian Game, Ruy Lopez, Scotch Game, Four Knights, Petroff,
  Vienna, and sidelines. Learn central breaks with d4, the f7 pressure point,
  open-file development, and quiet maneuvering in closed Ruy structures.
- **Sicilian Defense:** Open Sicilian as the theoretical main course, or one
  deliberate anti-Sicilian system. Learn Najdorf, Dragon, Classical,
  Scheveningen, Sveshnikov, Accelerated Dragon, Taimanov, Kan, and Kalashnikov
  structures. Know the Rossolimo, Moscow, Alapin, Closed Sicilian, and Grand
  Prix as major alternatives.
- **French Defense:** Advance, Tarrasch, Classical, Winawer, Exchange, and
  Rubinstein structures. Understand the d4-e5 chain, c5 pressure, f6 break,
  light-squared bishop, and queenside counterplay.
- **Caro-Kann Defense:** Classical, Advance, Panov, Exchange, Fantasy, and
  Two Knights systems. Understand c5/e5 breaks, development of the light bishop,
  and common favorable endings for Black.
- **Pirc and Modern:** Austrian Attack, Classical, 150 Attack, and restrained
  systems. Learn when the center is stable and when Black strikes with c5 or e5.
- **Scandinavian:** 2...Qxd5 and 2...Nf6 systems, queen-development tempi,
  and solid Caro-Kann-like structures.
- **Alekhine Defense:** Four Pawns, Modern, and Exchange variations; space
  advantage versus targetable center.
- **Minor defenses:** Owen's Defense, Nimzowitsch Defense, St. George, and
  uncommon first moves. Respond with development and principled central control.

Good primary choices by learning goal:

- Italian Game for development, tactics, and maneuvering.
- Ruy Lopez for a complete strategic education and heavier theory.
- Scotch for open positions and more direct play.
- Open Sicilian for maximum theoretical and dynamic training.
- Alapin or Rossolimo/Moscow package for lower maintenance, but not zero theory.

### 1.d4

Core responses to prepare:

- **Queen's Gambit Declined:** Orthodox, Tartakower, Lasker, Cambridge Springs,
  Ragozin, Vienna, and related structures.
- **Slav and Semi-Slav:** Main Slav, Chebanenko, Exchange Slav, Meran,
  Anti-Meran, Botvinnik, Moscow, and Anti-Moscow variations.
- **Nimzo-Indian:** Classical, Rubinstein, Sämisch, Leningrad, and f3 systems;
  bishop pair versus structure and dark-square control.
- **Queen's Indian and Bogo-Indian:** Development, queenside control, and
  flexible central play.
- **King's Indian:** Classical, Sämisch, Fianchetto, Four Pawns, and Averbakh;
  opposite-wing plans and locked-center timing.
- **Grünfeld:** Exchange, Russian, f3, and sidelines; central pawn mass versus
  piece pressure.
- **Benoni and Benko Gambit:** Modern Benoni structures, Taimanov attack,
  Fianchetto systems, and Benko queenside compensation.
- **Dutch Defense:** Leningrad, Stonewall, and Classical systems.
- **Queen's Gambit Accepted:** Central control, isolated queen's pawn, and
  active-piece positions.
- **Chigorin, Albin, Budapest, Englund, and other sidelines:** Know the sound
  refutation of traps and the resulting development plans.

Good primary choices by learning goal:

- Queen's Gambit for classical central and structural play.
- Catalan for long-term pressure and positional complexity.
- London as a lower-maintenance system, provided it is studied as structures
  and move orders rather than automatic moves.
- Trompowsky or Torre as secondary systems, not substitutes for chess thinking.

### 1.c4 and 1.Nf3

- English Opening: Symmetrical English, Reversed Sicilian, Four Knights,
  Botvinnik setup, and early ...e5 or ...c5 structures.
- Réti: double fianchetto, pressure on d5, delayed central occupation, and
  transpositions.
- Catalan and Queen's Gambit transpositions.
- Anti-Grünfeld, anti-Nimzo, and anti-King's Indian move orders.
- Hedgehog structures and typical b5/d5 breaks.

These moves reward transpositional knowledge. Advanced players must know not
only what position they want, but which move orders permit or prevent it.

## Black repertoire families against 1.e4

Choose one primary defense and learn one emergency alternative later.

### 1...e5

- Against the Ruy Lopez: Closed Spanish, Marshall, Berlin, Open Spanish,
  Archangel, and early sidelines.
- Against the Italian: Giuoco Piano, Two Knights, Evans Gambit, and quiet d3
  systems.
- Scotch, Four Knights, Vienna, King's Gambit, Bishop's Opening, Center Game,
  Danish Gambit, and Ponziani.
- Core skills: rapid development, central tension, tactical calculation, and
  maneuvering in symmetrical structures.

### Sicilian Defense

- Najdorf: ...e5 and ...e6 structures, English Attack, Bg5, Be2, and positional
  systems.
- Dragon and Accelerated Dragon: Yugoslav Attack, Maroczy Bind, exchange
  sacrifices, and long-diagonal play.
- Classical: Richter-Rauzer, Sozin, and Scheveningen transpositions.
- Sveshnikov and Kalashnikov: d5 outpost, weak d6 square, activity, and dynamic
  bishop pair.
- Taimanov and Kan: flexible ...e6/...a6 structures, hedgehog play, and move
  orders.
- Anti-Sicilians: Alapin, Rossolimo, Moscow, Closed, Grand Prix, Smith-Morra,
  Wing Gambit, and early sidelines.

### French Defense

- Winawer, Classical, Rubinstein, MacCutcheon, Burn, and Fort Knox options.
- Tarrasch, Advance, Exchange, King's Indian Attack, and sidelines.
- Core skills: pawn-chain attack, space disadvantage, counterbreak timing, and
  activating the light-squared bishop.

### Caro-Kann Defense

- Classical, Karpov, Bronstein-Larsen, and early ...c5 approaches.
- Advance with ...Bf5 or ...c5, Panov, Exchange, Fantasy, Two Knights, and
  sidelines.
- Core skills: sound structure, timely c5/e5 breaks, and endgame transitions.

### Pirc, Modern, and Alekhine

- Treat these as dynamic counterattacking defenses, not automatic systems.
- Learn the opponent's strongest space-gaining setups.
- Identify the exact moment to challenge the center.
- Accept that a delayed or mistimed break can leave Black strategically lost.

## Black repertoire families against 1.d4, 1.c4, and 1.Nf3

Choose a compatible system and map its transpositions.

### Queen's Gambit Declined family

- Orthodox, Lasker, Tartakower, Cambridge Springs, Ragozin, and Vienna.
- Exchange variation and minority attack.
- Catalan, Bf4 systems, and sidelines.
- Compatible responses to Réti and English move orders.

### Slav and Semi-Slav family

- Main Slav, ...a6 Slav, Exchange Slav, and early e3 systems.
- Semi-Slav Meran, Anti-Meran, Botvinnik, Moscow, and Anti-Moscow.
- Catalan and move-order choices involving ...c6, ...e6, and ...d5.

### Nimzo-Indian complex

- Nimzo-Indian against 3.Nc3.
- Queen's Indian, Bogo-Indian, or QGD against 3.Nf3.
- Catalan and 1.c4/1.Nf3 transpositions.
- Core tradeoff: structural damage and dark-square control versus bishop pair.

### King's Indian family

- Classical, Sämisch, Fianchetto, Averbakh, Four Pawns, and h3 systems.
- English and Réti transpositions.
- Core skills: closed-center attacks, pawn-break timing, and dynamic risk.

### Grünfeld family

- Exchange, Russian, f3, Bf4, Fianchetto, and sidelines.
- Anti-Grünfeld move orders from 1.c4 and 1.Nf3.
- Core skills: attack the pawn center concretely and know forcing theory.

### Dutch family

- Leningrad, Stonewall, and Classical.
- Anti-Dutch systems: 2.Bg5, Staunton Gambit, early h4, and queenside fianchetto.
- Core skills: kingside initiative, e5 control, and king-safety management.

### Benoni and Benko family

- Modern Benoni main structures, early f4, Fianchetto, and h3 systems.
- Benko accepted, declined, and modern move orders.
- Core skills: queenside activity, dark-square pressure, and long-term
  compensation.

## Opening-theory assessment

For each repertoire branch, the player must be able to:

- Play the first critical phase against a sparring partner from memory.
- Explain why each move is played.
- Name the first major pawn break for both sides.
- Identify the standard tactical trap for both sides.
- Annotate one model game without copying notes.
- Recover reasonably after an unfamiliar but sound sideline.
- State which ending each side welcomes.

If the player remembers a move but cannot explain the position, the branch is
not learned.

# Part III: Stage-by-stage program

## Stage A: Absolute beginner

**Suggested length:** 4-6 weeks  
**Typical entry:** No prior chess experience to approximately 600  
**Primary objective:** Play a complete legal game and understand every basic
way a game can end.

### Units

1. **Board and coordinates:** Orientation, files, ranks, squares, center, wings,
   and initial setup.
2. **Piece movement:** Legal movement, capture, blocking, and knight jumps.
3. **Pawn rules:** First two-square move, diagonal capture, en passant, and all
   promotion choices.
4. **King safety:** Check, attacked squares, three responses to check, and
   illegal self-check.
5. **Castling:** Both sides, every condition, and common illegal attempts.
6. **Game endings:** Checkmate, stalemate, resignation, time, and elementary
   draw concepts.
7. **Notation:** Read and write simple algebraic moves, captures, checks, mates,
   castling, and promotion.
8. **First mates:** Mate in one, ladder mate, and queen-plus-king mate.
9. **First games:** Guided games with pauses after every check, capture, and
   threat.

### Required practice

- 10 minutes of coordinate and movement drills on four days each week.
- 30 mate-in-one positions per week.
- 2 guided games and 2 independent games per week.
- 2 repetitions each of queen mate and ladder mate per week.
- Record at least one game in algebraic notation each week.

### Exit standards

- Set up the board correctly in under one minute.
- Make every special move legally and explain its conditions.
- Identify checkmate and stalemate correctly in 18 of 20 positions.
- Play three consecutive complete games without an illegal move.
- Mate with king and queen against a lone king without stalemating.
- Read and record a short game in basic algebraic notation.

## Stage B: Novice player

**Suggested length:** 6-10 weeks  
**Typical entry:** 600-900  
**Primary objective:** Protect material, recognize immediate threats, and use a
simple process on every move.

### Units

1. **Material and exchanges:** Piece values, equal trades, favorable trades,
   attackers, and defenders.
2. **Board scan:** Checks, captures, threats, hanging pieces, and the opponent's
   last-move idea.
3. **Basic tactics:** Fork, pin, skewer, double attack, discovery, and removal
   of defender.
4. **Mating patterns:** Back rank, smothered shape, queen-and-bishop battery,
   rook-and-queen ladder, and simple mating nets.
5. **Opening goals:** Center, development, castling, queen restraint, and
   connecting rooks.
6. **Starter repertoire:** Italian positions as White, 1...e5 against 1.e4, and
   1...d5 against 1.d4.
7. **Endgame basics:** Rook mate, king activation, rule of square, opposition,
   and basic promotion races.
8. **Game review:** Find the first lost piece and the first missed forcing move.
9. **Clock habits:** Pause after the opponent moves and preserve enough time to
   blunder-check.

### Required practice

- 75-100 easy tactical positions per week.
- 20 mate-in-one or mate-in-two positions per week.
- 2 games at 15+10 or slower per week.
- 2 guided reviews of personal games per week.
- Practice queen mate and rook mate from random legal starting positions.
- Replay one short annotated game each week.

### Exit standards

- Score 85% on 40 one-move tactical positions with mixed themes.
- Name the opponent's direct threat in at least 8 of 10 test positions.
- Complete five consecutive slow games without an unforced queen loss.
- Demonstrate king-and-queen and king-and-rook mates.
- Explain the five main opening goals without naming a memorized sequence.
- Use the rule of the square and direct opposition correctly.

## Stage C: Developing player

**Suggested length:** 8-12 weeks  
**Typical entry:** 900-1200  
**Primary objective:** Combine tactical safety, principled development, and
basic technique into a repeatable game process.

### Units

1. **Thinking routine:** Opponent threat, forcing moves, candidates, calculation,
   evaluation, and final blunder check.
2. **Two-move tactics:** Combinations using deflection, overload, discovered
   attack, back-rank weakness, and removing defenders.
3. **Tactical defense:** Meet mate threats, create luft, exchange attackers,
   counterattack, and return material when needed.
4. **Opening principles in context:** When to break a rule, how to punish slow
   development, and how to enter a playable middlegame.
5. **Compact repertoire:** Responses to the four main Black defenses after 1.e4
   or the principal defenses after 1.d4, plus Black plans against both first
   moves.
6. **Middlegame basics:** Open files, outposts, pawn weaknesses, good and bad
   bishops, and improving the least active piece.
7. **Attacking basics:** Bring more pieces than defenders, open lines, avoid
   premature sacrifices, and calculate every check.
8. **Endgame technique:** Key squares, protected passers, outside passers,
   triangulation introduction, and simple rook activity.
9. **Self-analysis:** Annotate thoughts and identify the first critical mistake
   before using an engine.
10. **Tournament readiness:** Touch-move, notation, clock, draw rules, and how to
    ask an arbiter for help.

### Required practice

- 100 mixed easy and moderate tactical positions per week.
- 4 calculation positions per week, spending 5-10 minutes on each.
- 2 games at 30 minutes plus increment or slower per week when possible.
- 3 basic endgame positions played from both sides per week.
- 1 independent personal-game analysis per week.
- 2 model games per month from the chosen starter repertoire.

### Exit standards

- Score 80% on 50 mixed one- and two-move tactical positions.
- Complete ten slow games with a visible thinking routine and materially fewer
  one-move piece losses.
- Reach a safe, developed position by move 10 in at least 8 of 10 games without
  relying on exact memorization.
- Demonstrate queen mate, rook mate, rule of square, opposition, key squares,
  and a basic pawn breakthrough.
- Explain open files, outposts, weak pawns, and king activity using examples.
- Analyze one complete game and identify the first three critical decisions.

## Stage 1: Intermediate foundations

**Suggested length:** 12-16 weeks  
**Typical entry:** 1200-1500 OTB-equivalent  
**Primary objective:** Eliminate avoidable losses and build a complete thinking
process.

### Units

1. **Board vision and safety:** attacked pieces, loose pieces, legal-move scan,
   opponent threats, and final blunder check.
2. **Core tactics:** forks, pins, skewers, discoveries, double attacks, removal
   of defender, back-rank patterns, and basic mating nets.
3. **Calculation basics:** forcing candidates and lines of 2-4 half-moves.
4. **Opening principles:** development, central influence, king safety,
   connection of rooks, and punishment of premature queen moves.
5. **One compact repertoire:** reach playable positions against all main first
   moves without memorizing long theory.
6. **Pawn structures:** open, closed, fixed, and fluid centers; isolated,
   doubled, backward, and passed pawns.
7. **Piece activity:** worst piece, open files, outposts, good and bad bishops.
8. **Basic endings:** king activation, opposition, key squares, rule of square,
   Lucena, Philidor, and fundamental mates.
9. **Practical play:** notation, clock use, touch-move, draw claims, and game
   analysis.

### Required practice

- 100-150 tactical problems per week, mostly easy enough for pattern fluency.
- 2 slow games per week or one slow game plus two 15+10 games.
- 3 basic endgame positions played from both sides per week.
- 1 deeply analyzed personal game per week.
- 1 annotated model game per week.

### Exit standards

- Score 85% on 50 mixed core tactical patterns.
- Complete 10 consecutive rapid or slow games with no undefended one-move piece
  loss.
- Demonstrate queen mate, rook mate, opposition, key squares, Lucena, and
  Philidor without notes.
- Explain the plan after the opening in every main repertoire branch.
- Produce a complete self-analysis that identifies at least three critical
  moments before engine use.

## Stage 2: Strong intermediate

**Suggested length:** 16-24 weeks  
**Typical entry:** 1500-1800  
**Primary objective:** Calculate accurately and connect tactics to structure.

### Units

1. **Expanded tactics:** deflection, attraction, interference, clearance,
   overloading, zwischenzug, trapped pieces, defensive tactics, and sacrifices.
2. **Calculation:** candidate comparison, visualization, quiet moves, and
   opponent's best defense.
3. **Attacking play:** prerequisites for attack, Greek Gift, open lines,
   opposite-side castling, and attack-to-endgame transitions.
4. **Defense:** exchange attackers, close lines, return material, and create
   counterplay.
5. **Structural play:** isolated queen's pawn, hanging pawns, Carlsbad, French
   chain, open Sicilian, and one structure from the personal repertoire.
6. **Planning:** improve worst piece, use pawn breaks, create a second weakness,
   and distinguish temporary from permanent advantages.
7. **Endgames:** practical rook endings, minor-piece endings, pawn races,
   triangulation, wrong bishop and rook pawn, and opposite-colored bishops.
8. **Opening literacy:** main branches to 10-12 moves, traps, model games, and
   typical endings.
9. **Analysis discipline:** compare independent analysis, reference material,
   and engine findings.

### Required practice

- 60-100 mixed tactics weekly, with full calculation rather than guessing.
- 6 calculation positions weekly at 10-20 minutes each.
- 4 endgame positions weekly, played against resistance.
- 4 model games per month from the player's openings.
- 4-6 serious games per month.
- Monthly error-log review and training-plan adjustment.

### Exit standards

- Score 80% on 40 mixed tactical and defensive problems with written lines.
- Calculate at least four half-moves accurately in forcing positions and give a
  defensible final evaluation.
- Demonstrate all core pawn endings and the main rook-versus-rook-and-pawn
  methods in the syllabus.
- Explain both sides' plans in six major pawn structures.
- Maintain a repertoire file that covers at least 90% of positions reached in
  the player's last 20 serious games by move 8.

## Stage 3: Advanced club player

**Suggested length:** 20-32 weeks  
**Typical entry:** 1800-2000  
**Primary objective:** Make accurate strategic decisions backed by concrete
calculation.

### Units

1. **Calculation depth:** complex trees, only-move defense, comparison exercises,
   and evaluation at quiescent nodes.
2. **Positional tactics:** exchange sacrifices, domination, restriction,
   breakthrough, and transformation.
3. **Prophylaxis:** opponent's plan, prevention, flexible moves, and limiting
   counterplay.
4. **Imbalances:** bishop pair, exchange imbalance, queen versus pieces,
   material versus initiative, and color complexes.
5. **Advanced structures:** Hedgehog, Maroczy, Benoni, King's Indian, Catalan,
   and repertoire-specific structures.
6. **Endgames:** Vancura, short-side defense, rook pawn exceptions, queen
   endings, rook versus minor piece, and advanced bishop-versus-knight play.
7. **Opening tabiyas:** critical positions, transpositions, novelty detection,
   and model-game clusters.
8. **Tournament skill:** preparation, rest, nutrition, round review, and
   emotional reset.
9. **Opponent preparation:** style, repertoire, recurring structures, and
   practical line selection.

### Required practice

- 8 calculation positions weekly, including two defensive positions.
- 2 blindfold or visualization fragments weekly.
- 5 theoretical endgames tested weekly.
- 1 opening tabiya researched and summarized weekly.
- 6 classical or long rapid games monthly.
- 1 annotated master game reconstructed from memory every two weeks.

### Exit standards

- Produce reliable 6-10 half-move calculation in suitable forcing positions.
- Defend five difficult positions against a stronger player or engine with
  reasonable practical resistance.
- Explain ten core structures and locate representative model games.
- Demonstrate the complete core rook-endgame syllabus from both sides.
- Show a 30-game sample with materially reduced losses from opening surprises,
  time trouble, and single-move blunders.

## Stage 4: Expert and candidate master

**Suggested length:** 24-40 weeks  
**Typical entry:** 2000-2200  
**Primary objective:** Achieve consistent expert performance and prepare for
master-strength competition.

### Units

1. **High-complexity calculation:** broad candidate trees, concealed tactics,
   long forcing lines, and exact defensive resources.
2. **Strategic precision:** prophylaxis, transformation, subtle exchanges,
   multi-stage plans, and playing without obvious targets.
3. **Technical conversion:** second weakness, domination, restriction, and
   conversion without allowing active counterplay.
4. **Advanced defense:** fortresses, perpetual mechanisms, exchange-down
   resistance, queen-ending checks, and active rook defense.
5. **Complete endgame core:** advanced rook, queen, mixed-material, and
   reciprocal-zugzwang positions.
6. **Professional repertoire management:** engine-assisted analysis, database
   hygiene, transposition tables, uncertainty labels, and update scheduling.
7. **Opponent and event preparation:** choose lines based on standing, color,
   opponent, rest, and tournament format.
8. **Performance analysis:** centipawn loss is secondary to decision quality,
   clock context, and repeatable error categories.
9. **Competitive psychology:** resilience, draw offers, rating pressure, tilt,
   and disciplined risk.

### Required practice

- 10 demanding calculation positions weekly, half without a tactical cue.
- 6 endgame positions weekly under a clock.
- 2 complete master games weekly, one from the player's repertoire and one from
  a weakness area.
- One repertoire maintenance block weekly.
- One tournament or concentrated match block every 6-10 weeks when feasible.
- Quarterly review with a qualified coach or strong peer.

### Exit standards

- Score at least 70% against a calibrated set of expert-level mixed positions.
- Produce coherent independent annotations that survive engine review at most
  critical moments.
- Demonstrate practical competence across every endgame family in this
  curriculum, even when exact theoretical recall is incomplete.
- Enter serious games with an opponent-specific plan and post-game preparation
  audit.
- Sustain approximately 2200-level OTB performance over a meaningful event
  sample or meet an equivalent coach-approved performance standard.

## Stage 5: Master preparation

**Suggested length:** Ongoing, organized in 8-12 week event cycles  
**Typical entry:** 2200+  
**Primary objective:** Turn broad competence into master-level consistency and
results.

### Individualized program

At this stage, a fixed generic schedule becomes less useful. Build each cycle
from the player's game database.

1. Classify the last 50-100 serious games by opening, structure, result,
   critical error, time use, and opponent strength.
2. Select the three highest-cost recurring weaknesses.
3. Preserve strengths while devoting at least 60% of study time to those
   weaknesses.
4. Plan around target events and likely opponents.
5. Test changes in training games before using them in a title event.
6. Taper volume before competition while preserving tactical sharpness.

### Master-level competencies

- Generate strong candidates in unfamiliar positions.
- Calculate deeply while preserving accuracy and clock balance.
- Evaluate compensation and long-term imbalances without relying on slogans.
- Know when to reject an engine's impractical first choice for a robust human
  alternative, while understanding the objective cost.
- Maintain a sound, current, and flexible opening repertoire.
- Prepare targeted ideas rather than merely memorizing engine lines.
- Convert small advantages and defend inferior endings for many moves.
- Recognize fortresses, tablebase transitions, and tactical exceptions.
- Recover between rounds and avoid emotional spillover.
- Use draws and risk appropriately for event goals without violating sporting
  ethics.

### Event-cycle template

| Phase | Duration | Focus |
|---|---:|---|
| Audit | 1 week | Review games, choose weaknesses, set metrics |
| Build | 3-5 weeks | Calculation, technical repair, repertoire work |
| Apply | 2-3 weeks | Training matches, opening sparring, timed endings |
| Simulate | 1 week | Event time control and schedule |
| Taper | 3-5 days | Light tactics, key files, sleep, logistics |
| Compete | Event | Execute routines and record decisions |
| Debrief | 1-3 days | Recover first, then analyze and update plan |

### Exit and continuation standards

There is no final graduation from chess. A player reaches the curriculum's
master objective when they demonstrate master-strength performance in serious
competition and satisfy the relevant federation's title rules. Continue with
individualized cycles for FM, IM, and GM goals.

# Part IV: Course sequence

## 78-module beginner-to-master sequence

Coaches may teach one module per week at early stages and two to four weeks per
module at advanced stages.

### Modules B1-B18: Beginner pathway

B1. Board orientation, coordinates, setup, and piece names.  
B2. Rook, bishop, queen, and knight movement and capture.  
B3. King movement, attacked squares, check, and legal responses.  
B4. Pawns, two-square moves, capture, en passant, and promotion.  
B5. Castling and every condition that makes it legal or illegal.

# Part V: Teaching and assessment system

## Lesson template

Each 60-90 minute lesson should contain:

1. **Retrieval warm-up, 10 minutes:** Review prior positions without notes.
2. **Concept, 15 minutes:** Explain one idea with two clear examples.
3. **Guided practice, 15 minutes:** Student verbalizes candidates and
   calculation.
4. **Independent test, 20 minutes:** New positions with no theme label.
5. **Application, 15 minutes:** Play a thematic position or analyze a game.
6. **Summary, 5 minutes:** Student states the rule, exception, and action item.

## Assessment types

- **Recognition test:** Name or describe the motif.
- **Calculation test:** Write full variations and final evaluations.
- **Execution test:** Play the position against active resistance.
- **Transfer test:** Solve an unfamiliar position that combines themes.
- **Retention test:** Repeat after one week and one month.
- **Performance test:** Show improvement in serious games.

### Scoring rubric

| Score | Description |
|---:|---|
| 4 | Correct move, accurate line, sound evaluation, and clear explanation |
| 3 | Correct move and main idea with a minor calculation or explanation flaw |
| 2 | Partial idea but incomplete or materially inaccurate execution |
| 1 | Theme recognized only after prompting |
| 0 | No useful recognition or illegal analysis |

A module is mastered at an average of 3.2 or higher, with no core position below
2, on two separated assessments.

## Portfolio requirements

Maintain these artifacts throughout the program:

- Personal game database with full clock times when available.
- Error log and monthly summaries.
- Repertoire file with model games and update dates.
- Endgame position deck.
- Tactical failure deck.
- Annotated-game portfolio.
- Quarterly performance report with goals and evidence.

## Coach review checklist

Every 8-12 weeks, review:

- Rating and performance rating, with sample size.
- Results by color and time control.
- Opening positions reached and preparation quality.
- Error frequency and cost by tag.
- Calculation accuracy and time.
- Endgame results from equal, better, and worse positions.
- Clock distribution across the game.
- Training adherence, sleep, stress, and tournament load.
- Next cycle's three priorities and explicit depriorities.

## Puzzle-training rules

- Use untimed pattern drills for fluency and timed mixed sets for decision
  training.
- Do not move until the full line and final evaluation are clear.
- Do not assume every puzzle has a win. Include defense, equality, and best
  practical move positions.
- Repeat failed positions after 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months.
- Track false positives, where a sacrifice looked attractive but was unsound.
- Stop a session when fatigue turns deliberate calculation into guessing.

## Model-game study method

1. Play quickly through the whole game once.
2. Identify the opening structure and likely plans.
3. Hide the moves at 3-5 critical points and choose a move.
4. Compare the player's move with your candidates.
5. Explain tactical and strategic reasons in your own words.
6. Replay the game from memory the next day.
7. Add only the most reusable positions to the review deck.

# Part VII: Tournament and professional practice

## Tournament rules and conduct

Players should know:

- Touch-move and completion of a move.
- Correct clock operation and time-control transitions.
- Recording requirements and when a player may stop recording.
- Illegal-move procedure.
- Draw by agreement, repetition, 50-move rule, 75-move rule, stalemate, and
  insufficient material.
- How and when to summon an arbiter.
- Electronic-device and anti-cheating restrictions.
- Rapid, blitz, and classical rule differences.
- Respectful conduct and the prohibition on outside assistance.

Use the current
[FIDE Rules Commission documentation](https://rcc.fide.com/documentation/)
rather than relying on memory, since event rules can change.

## Pre-event checklist

- Confirm schedule, time control, venue, travel, equipment, and rules.
- Adjust sleep to round times.
- Review a small set of likely opening branches, not the entire repertoire.
- Complete tactical work at normal intensity, then taper.
- Define process goals: clock checkpoints, emotional reset, and blunder scan.
- Avoid untested repertoire changes immediately before the event.

## Between rounds

- Record key thoughts while fresh, but postpone exhaustive analysis.
- Eat, hydrate, walk, and disengage briefly.
- Review only likely next-round openings.
- Do not let a win create overconfidence or a loss create revenge play.
- Protect sleep over late-night engine analysis.

## Ethical engine use

- Never use an engine, database, notes, or outside assistance during a game
  unless the specific correspondence rules explicitly permit it.
- In study, analyze independently first.
- Treat engine evaluation as evidence, not explanation.
- Explore alternatives until you understand the mechanism behind the number.
- Preserve uncertainty in repertoire notes when analysis is inconclusive.

# Part IX: Completion checklist

The player has completed the broad curriculum when they can demonstrate all of
the following, even though continued refinement never ends.

## Rules and foundations

- [ ] Set up and orient the board correctly.
- [ ] Move every piece legally, including castling, en passant, and promotion.
- [ ] Distinguish check, checkmate, stalemate, and the principal draw rules.
- [ ] Read and write algebraic notation.
- [ ] Execute king-and-queen and king-and-rook mates.
- [ ] Use a full-board safety scan and identify hanging pieces.
- [ ] Apply opening principles without making automatic moves.

## Tactics and calculation

- [ ] Recognize and calculate every core tactical family in this curriculum.
- [ ] Find defensive resources and tactical draws, not only winning attacks.
- [ ] Generate candidates systematically in quiet and forcing positions.
- [ ] Visualize long lines without losing track of pieces.
- [ ] Evaluate the final position rather than stopping after material gain.

## Strategy

- [ ] Evaluate positions using concrete imbalances.
- [ ] Form plans from pawn structures and piece placement.
- [ ] Use prophylaxis and identify the opponent's plan.
- [ ] Choose favorable exchanges and reject harmful simplification.
- [ ] Convert advantages and defend inferior positions actively.

## Openings

- [ ] Maintain a complete White and Black repertoire.
- [ ] Understand structures, breaks, tactics, and endings in every branch.
- [ ] Handle main lines, sidelines, move orders, and transpositions.
- [ ] Use model games, databases, and engines responsibly.
- [ ] Prepare for specific opponents without overfitting.

## Endgames

- [ ] Execute all fundamental mates.
- [ ] Solve core pawn endings using exact calculation.
- [ ] Demonstrate essential rook-ending positions and defensive methods.
- [ ] Play minor-piece, queen, and mixed-material endings competently.
- [ ] Recognize fortresses, stalemate, perpetual check, and transition risks.

## Competitive practice

- [ ] Know the current rules and notation requirements.
- [ ] Manage the clock across a full tournament game.
- [ ] Analyze games independently before using an engine.
- [ ] Maintain an error log and adapt training from evidence.
- [ ] Prepare, compete, recover, and debrief through a complete event cycle.

# Appendix A: One-page daily decision checklist

Before the opponent moves:

- What are their forcing ideas?
- Which pieces or squares are loose?
- What changed after the last move?

After the opponent moves:

1. Is my king in check?
2. What does the move attack, defend, open, close, or abandon?
3. What checks, captures, and threats exist for both sides?
4. What are my two to four candidates?
5. What is their best reply to each candidate?
6. What does the final position evaluate to?
7. Before moving: is the move legal, safe, and vulnerable to a forcing reply?

# Appendix D: Curriculum maintenance

Review this curriculum every six months. Opening recommendations, title
regulations, platform features, and competitive rules may change. The tactical,
calculation, strategic, and theoretical-endgame core changes much more slowly,
but examples and training methods should still be updated from student results.

When revising:

1. Preserve learning outcomes and assessment evidence.
2. Replace stale opening lines and broken links.
3. Add a topic only if it changes training or assessment.
4. Remove duplication that does not improve spaced learning.
5. Keep the player's personal plan narrower than the full reference curriculum.
