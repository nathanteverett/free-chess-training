## Overview

A chessboard has to be turned the correct way before a single move is played: each player must see a light square in their own bottom-right corner. Get this wrong and every square name, every diagram you copy, and every pattern you memorise is mirrored. The rule is tiny, it takes two seconds to check, and it is the foundation for reading and writing chess moves for the rest of your life.

## The rule and why it exists

The 64 squares alternate light and dark. Turn the board a quarter turn and the colour in any given corner flips. So the light-square-on-the-right rule is what fixes the board in place, and once it is fixed the coordinates follow.

The files, the up-and-down columns, are lettered a to h from White's left to White's right. The ranks, the side-to-side rows, are numbered 1 to 8 starting from White's side. Every square is a file letter plus a rank number: a1 in White's bottom-left corner, h1 in White's bottom-right, a8 in the far top-left, h8 in the far top-right. h1 is light. So is a8. And a1 and h8 are dark. The old saying is "white on right", and a longer version is "a1 is dark".

This is not decoration. The whole language of chess depends on it. When a book says the bishop on c1 is a dark-squared bishop, that is only true because c1 is dark, and c1 is only dark because the board is set the standard way.

<Spoken>Reading the bottom rank from White's left to right: a1 is dark, b1 is light, c1 is dark, d1 is light, e1 is dark, f1 is light, g1 is dark, and h1 is light.</Spoken>

## Placing the pieces

Once the board is oriented, the back rank fills in from the corners. Rooks go in the corners: a1 and h1 for White, a8 and h8 for Black. Knights go next to them on b1 and g1, then bishops on c1 and f1. That leaves d1 and e1 for the queen and king.

Here is the part beginners get wrong: the queen goes on her own colour. White's queen, a white piece, starts on d1, which is a light square. Black's queen starts on d8, which is a dark square. The kings take what is left, e1 and e8. Notice the consequence: both queens sit on the d-file facing each other, and both kings sit on the e-file facing each other. If your queen and king are not staring straight down the board at their opposite numbers, one of them is misplaced.

The pawns then fill the second rank for White, a2 through h2, and the seventh rank for Black, a7 through h7.

## What breaks when the board is wrong

Suppose you turn the board a quarter turn so that a dark square sits bottom right. Now everything is mirrored left-to-right. Kingside becomes queenside. The natural opening move 1.e4, which frees the light-squared bishop toward c4 and the knight toward f3, would come out as a move on the d-file instead.

Castling is where the damage really shows. Kingside castling, written 0-0, sends the white king from e1 to g1 and the rook from h1 to f1. It is short because there are only two squares between the king and the h1 rook. If your king and rook are on the wrong wings, you will "castle" into a position that no book, no video and no engine agrees with.

The same goes for patterns. The scholar's-mate try 1.e4 e5 2.Bc4 Nc6 3.Qh5 Nf6 4.Qxf7 is checkmate because f7 is defended only by the black king. That single fact, that f7 and f2 are the weakest squares in the starting position, is a coordinate fact. Mirror the board and you would go hunting for a weakness on c7 that is not there.

## Building coordinate fluency

Knowing the rule is not the same as knowing the board. Strong players see a square name and the colour arrives instantly. That skill is trainable.

Start with the colour trick. A square is light when its file letter and rank number are of opposite parity, one odd and one even. a, c, e, g are the odd letters, first, third, fifth, seventh in the alphabet. So a1 (odd letter, odd number) is dark, and h5 (even letter, odd number) is light. Test yourself: f6 is even and even, so dark. c4 is odd and even, so light. The knight on the standard tour from b1 to c3 to d5 goes dark, light, dark? Check it: b1 is light, c3 is dark, d5 is light. Knights always change square colour with every move, which is why that alternation is a useful self-check.

Then practise naming squares out loud while looking away from the board, and practise finding the diagonals. The long light diagonal runs a2-b3-c4-d5-e6-f7-g8, plus a8 and h1 on the other long one. The long dark diagonal runs a1-b2-c3-d4-e5-f6-g7-h8. A fianchettoed bishop on g2, reached after 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6 3.g3 Bg7, is powerful precisely because it owns that long dark diagonal from g7 down toward b2 and beyond.

Finally, learn to see the board from Black's side. Play a few games as Black on a physical set. Everything is upside down, a8 is now in your bottom-right area, and you will discover how much of your coordinate knowledge was really just picture memory.

## Common mistakes

- Setting up with a dark square bottom right. Check the corner nearest your right hand before touching a piece; if it is dark, rotate the board a quarter turn.
- Swapping the king and queen. The queen goes on her own colour: white queen on d1 (light), black queen on d8 (dark). Both queens end up on the d-file, both kings on the e-file, each facing its counterpart.
- Putting the bishops in the corners or the knights outside the rooks. From the corner inward the order is always rook, knight, bishop.
- Assuming a1 is light because a-files and first ranks feel like a natural starting point. a1 is dark. Memorise that one square and you can rebuild the whole colour map from it.
- Learning coordinates only from White's point of view, then freezing when you sit on the black side of a real board. Deliberately spend sessions playing and studying from Black's perspective.
