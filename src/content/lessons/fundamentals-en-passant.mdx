## Overview

En passant is the rule that stops a pawn from sneaking past an enemy pawn by using its two-square first move. If a pawn advances two squares and lands beside an enemy pawn, that enemy pawn may capture it as though it had only moved one square. The capture is legal on the very next move and never again. It is the only capture in chess where the capturing piece does not land on the square of the piece it removes.

## The rule, stated exactly

Four conditions must all hold at once.

Your pawn must be on the fifth rank counting from your own side: rank 5 for White, rank 4 for Black. The enemy pawn must have just moved two squares in one go, from its starting rank. It must land directly beside your pawn on an adjacent file. And you must capture immediately, on your very next move.

Concrete case. You have a White pawn on e5. Black plays d7-d5, so the black pawn now sits on d5, side by side with your e5 pawn. You answer exd6. Your pawn moves diagonally to d6 — the square the black pawn skipped over — and the black pawn on d5 is removed from the board. Your pawn ends on d6, an empty square, and d5 is empty too.

The mirror image works for Black. A black pawn on f4 faces g2-g4. Black replies fxg3, landing on g3 and removing the pawn from g4.

Nothing else triggers it. A pawn that walks one square at a time is never subject to en passant, no matter where it stops. A knight, bishop, rook, queen or king that lands beside your pawn is not subject to it either. Only pawns capture en passant, and only pawns are captured en passant.

## Why the rule exists

Before the two-square move was introduced, pawns crawled one square at a time and a pawn on the fifth rank always got to say something about an enemy pawn trying to slip by on the next file. When the two-square opening move was added to speed the game up, it created a loophole: a pawn could jump from d7 straight to d5, sailing past the guarded square d6, and your e5 pawn could do nothing.

En passant closes that loophole. It restores the pawn's control of the square it was already watching. That is the whole idea, and if you remember it you will never misapply the rule: en passant lets your pawn capture on the square it already attacked.

## The one-move window

This is where almost every mistake happens. The right to capture en passant expires the instant you play any other move.

Say you have a pawn on e5 and Black plays d7-d5. You look at exd6, decide you would rather develop, and play Nf3. Black replies Nc6. Now the black pawn is still on d5, still beside your pawn, and exd6 is illegal. It is gone forever. The chance existed for exactly one move.

It is also optional. Nobody forces you to take. In many positions capturing en passant is simply bad — it can open a file toward your own king, hand your opponent a strong central pawn, or trade off a pawn that was cramping them. Judge it like any other capture.

Two consequences worth knowing. First, en passant is part of the position: two positions that look identical on the board are not the same for repetition purposes if en passant is available in one and not the other. That is why chess notation for a position records an en passant square. Second, because it is a legal move, en passant can be the move that rescues you from stalemate or breaks a zugzwang, and in king-and-pawn endings a passed pawn that races through on the fifth rank can be caught by it.

## Where it shows up in real games

In the French Defence, after 1.e4 e6, White can play the offbeat 2.e5, and Black challenges with 2...d5. White then has 3.exd6, capturing en passant, and after 3...Bxd6 or 3...Qxd6 Black has a comfortable game — an example of a legal en passant capture that is not especially strong.

The pattern that matters most in practice is the pawn chain break. Whenever White has a pawn on e5 in a French or Advance structure and Black tries to blast the chain open with f7-f5, White gets exf6 en passant and the black pawn on f5 disappears, often leaving a hole on e6 and a weak king. Black must decide before pushing whether recapturing on f6 with the knight, queen or g-pawn actually helps. The same logic runs in reverse in the King's Indian, where a black e5 pawn meeting f2-f4 can lead to structures where the two-square pawn advances collide.

## Common mistakes

- Trying to capture en passant a move late. The right lasts exactly one move. If you played anything else in between, the pawn beside you is now an ordinary pawn and can only be taken by a normal capture, which usually means never.
- Applying it to a one-square push. A pawn that steps d7-d6 and then d6-d5 has never skipped a square. Your e5 pawn cannot take it en passant, even though the pawns end up side by side.
- Trying to take a piece en passant. Only a pawn that has just made a two-square move can be captured this way. A knight landing on d5 beside your e5 pawn is not capturable en passant.
- Putting the capturing pawn on the wrong square. Your pawn lands on the square the enemy pawn passed over, not on the square the enemy pawn occupies. From e5 against a pawn on d5, you go to d6, not d5.
- Capturing automatically because you can. En passant is optional. Ask what it does to your structure, your king and your opponent's, exactly as you would with any other capture.
