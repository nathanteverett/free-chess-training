## Overview

A bishop moves any number of empty squares along a diagonal, in any of the four diagonal directions, and it captures by landing on an enemy piece that stands on that diagonal. Because every diagonal step keeps a square the same color, a bishop is locked onto the color it starts on for the entire game. That single fact shapes almost everything you need to know about bishops: which squares they can never touch, why the two of them together are so strong, and why a bishop stuck behind its own pawns is nearly useless.

## The movement rule, exactly

Put a bishop on d4. It can travel up-right along d4, e5, f6, g7, h8; up-left along c5, b6, a7; down-right along e3, f2, g1; and down-left along c3, b2, a1. That is thirteen squares, and every one of them is a dark square, just like d4.

The bishop is a sliding piece, so it cannot jump. If a piece of either color sits on e5, the bishop's whole up-right path is cut: f6, g7 and h8 all become unreachable that move. If the blocker is an enemy piece, the bishop may capture it by moving onto e5 and removing it from the board. If it is one of your own pieces, the bishop simply stops short and cannot go past. Captures always happen on the destination square. A bishop never captures something it merely passes over, and it never moves one way and captures another the way a pawn does.

Range depends heavily on where the bishop stands. In the center of an open board a bishop sees thirteen squares. Shoved into a corner on a1, it sees only seven. Bishops want the middle, or at least a long open diagonal.

## Light squares and dark squares

Each side starts with two bishops. White's begin on c1 (a dark square) and f1 (a light square); Black's on c8 (light) and f8 (dark). The c1-bishop will be a dark-squared bishop on move one and on move ninety. There is no promotion, no exception, no trick that changes it.

This is why the pair of bishops is worth more than the sum of its parts. Two bishops working together cover both colors, and with enough open lines they can control a huge amount of the board from a distance. If you trade one bishop off, every square of that color becomes a permanent weakness in your camp. When a player's dark-squared bishop disappears and the opponent's stays on, you often see attacks funnel straight into the holes: h6, g7 and f6 around a castled black king, for example.

It also means a lone bishop can never deliver checkmate by itself, even with a king helping, because half the board is invisible to it. King and bishop against a bare king is a draw by insufficient material.

## Good bishops and bad bishops

A bishop is only as good as the diagonals it can actually use. Your own pawns are the usual culprits. If your pawns sit on the same color as your bishop, they stand in its way; the bishop is called "bad." If your pawns sit on the opposite color, the bishop breathes; it is "good."

The French Defense is the classic illustration. After 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.e5, Black's pawns end up on light squares (d5, e6, and often f7), and the light-squared bishop on c8 is boxed in by its own structure. Black often spends several moves solving exactly that problem, with plans like c5, Qb6 and Bd7-b5 to trade the poor bishop off.

Compare that with the Italian Game: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4. That bishop rakes the a2-g8 diagonal and stares straight at f7, the square Black's king defends alone at the start. With a knight on g5 the pair can hit f7 twice, which is the whole idea behind lines like 4.Ng5 after Black plays 3...Nf6. The bishop is doing real work from three squares away, and it does that because nothing stands in its path.

The lesson: when you develop a bishop, ask what it is actually looking at. A bishop on e2 blocked by pawns on d3 and f3 is a spectator wearing a uniform.

## Fianchetto and long diagonals

One reliable way to give a bishop a career is the fianchetto: play g3 and Bg2 (or b3 and Bb2, or for Black g6 and Bg7, b6 and Bb7). The bishop takes up residence on the longest diagonal on the board, h1-a8 or a1-h8, eight squares of it, and often pressures the enemy center and queenside from behind its own pawn shelter.

The fianchettoed bishop on g7 also guards the dark squares around a castled king. Trading it away, or letting it get shut in, is a real concession. If you ever play g6 and Bg7 and then let the bishop be exchanged, watch the dark squares f6, g7 and h6 carefully. That is where the attack will come.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the bishop like a queen. It only moves diagonally. From f1 it can never reach f4, no matter how many empty squares lie between them. If a target is on a different color from your bishop, that bishop will never attack it, ever.
- Trying to hop over a blocker. A bishop on c1 with a pawn on d2 has zero moves along that diagonal. Beginners often plan Bc1-g5 and only then notice the d2-pawn. Move the pawn or the knight first.
- Developing a bishop to a square where it bites on granite. Bd3 in front of an unmoved d-pawn, or Bc4 into a solid pawn on d5 and e6, wastes the piece. Ask what diagonal it opens onto.
- Trading a bishop off casually, especially the one defending your king's color complex. If you have fianchettoed on g7, think twice before allowing an exchange on that square.
- Forgetting that the bishop pair needs open lines. Two bishops in a closed, locked pawn structure can be worse than two knights, which hop over everything. Open the position if you have the bishops; keep it closed if you have the knights.
