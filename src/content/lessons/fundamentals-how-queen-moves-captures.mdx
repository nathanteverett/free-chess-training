## Overview

The queen moves like a rook and a bishop at once: any number of empty squares in a straight line, forwards, backwards, sideways, or diagonally. She captures exactly the way she moves, by landing on an enemy piece and removing it from the board. That combination makes her the strongest piece on the board, worth roughly nine pawns, which also makes her the most expensive piece to lose.

## The eight directions

Put a white queen on d4 with the rest of the board empty and count her squares. Along the fourth rank she reaches a4, b4, c4, e4, f4, g4, h4. Up and down the d-file she reaches d1, d2, d3, d5, d6, d7, d8. On the long diagonal she runs a1, b2, c3 one way and e5, f6, g7, h8 the other. On the short diagonal she covers a7, b6, c5 and e3, f2, g1. That is twenty-seven squares from a single central square, more than a third of the board.

<Spoken>From d4 on an empty board the queen controls twenty-seven squares: the whole fourth rank, the whole d-file, and both diagonals through d4.</Spoken>

She is not a jumper. Every one of those lines stops the moment something blocks it. If a white pawn sits on d2, the queen on d4 cannot reach d1. If a black knight sits on f6, the queen can capture it but cannot continue to g7 or h8 on that move. Your own pieces block her completely; enemy pieces block her too, except that she may take the first one she meets.

Capturing is not a separate rule. There is no special queen capture, no sideways-only take, nothing like the pawn's diagonal exception. If the queen can travel to a square, and an enemy piece is standing there, she can take it.

## Counting a queen's real reach

Twenty-seven squares is the empty-board number. In a real game the queen's reach depends entirely on how open the lines are. On move one, the queen on d1 has zero legal moves: d2, c1, e1, c2, and e2 are all occupied by her own army. After 1.e4 she suddenly has five squares along the f1-a6 diagonal, from e2 all the way to h5.

That is why the queen gets stronger as pawns come off the board and files and diagonals open up. In the endgame a queen on a central square can hit both wings at once. In the opening she is often stuck behind her own men, and dragging her out early usually just gives your opponent free tempi.

Look at the classic beginner trap that follows from that reach. After 1.e4 e5 2.Qh5, White is threatening 2...Qxe5+ and also eyeing f7. If Black plays the natural-looking 2...Nc6 3.Bc4 and then blunders with 3...Nf6, White has 4.Qxf7 mate. The queen lands on f7, protected by the bishop on c4, and the black king has no square. The queen delivered mate on a diagonal she reached along a rank. That is the two-in-one power in one move.

But 2.Qh5 is not good play. Black simply answers 2...Nc6 3.Bc4 g6, hitting the queen, and after 4.Qf3 Nf6 Black is developing while White's queen shuffles around dodging pawns and knights.

## The queen as a forking machine

Because she attacks on lines and diagonals simultaneously, the queen forks more naturally than any other piece. A queen on e5 in an open position can attack an undefended rook on a1 down the long diagonal and an undefended bishop on h5 along the fifth rank in the same breath.

This cuts both ways. The queen is also the easiest piece to attack, because she must run from anything cheaper that touches her. A pawn, a knight, or a bishop that hits her wins time for free. She can capture a defended piece only when she is winning material by doing so, since trading a nine-point queen for a three-point knight that is guarded is a disaster.

The practical habit: before you move the queen anywhere, ask what pawn or minor piece could attack her from her new square next move.

## Queen versus the other pieces

Two rooks are worth about ten points on paper and can be stronger than a queen when they are connected and have open files. But a lone queen beats a lone rook, and queen plus king can force mate against a bare king easily, while a lone bishop or knight cannot.

Her value is not in raw squares, it is in what she can do with them: she can check a king from long range, defend a distant pawn while attacking on the other wing, and support a passed pawn all the way home. She just cannot do any of that if she is loose, trapped, or being chased.

## Common mistakes

- Bringing the queen out on move two or three. Every developing move Black plays hits her, and you lose tempo. Develop knights and bishops first; the queen usually comes out around move five to ten, to a safe square like e2, d2, c2, or b3.
- Forgetting that the queen cannot jump. Players see the h5 to e8 diagonal and forget the pawn on f7 stands in the way. Trace the line square by square.
- Grabbing a defended pawn. Taking a b2 pawn with the queen and getting the queen trapped is one of the most common ways to lose a game outright. Ask what defends the pawn, then ask how you would get out.
- Leaving the queen on a square that a knight can hit. Knights attack in a way you cannot see on lines. Check knight jumps specifically.
- Trading queens without thinking. If you are attacking the enemy king, the queen is your main attacker and the trade throws the attack away. If you are up material and defending, the trade is usually great.
