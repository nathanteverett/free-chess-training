## Overview

The rook is a sliding piece. It moves any number of squares in a straight line, either along a rank (side to side) or along a file (up and down), and it cannot jump over anything. It captures by landing on the square of the first enemy piece in its path, replacing that piece on the board. Learn to see the four straight lines shooting out of a rook and you will already spot half of the tactics beginners miss.

## The four rays

Put a white rook alone on d4. It can go up the d-file to d5, d6, d7 or d8. It can go down to d3, d2 or d1. It can go left along the fourth rank to c4, b4 or a4, and right to e4, f4, g4 or h4. That is fourteen squares. The rook never moves diagonally, so d5 and e5 are reachable but e5 is not reachable in one move from d4.

Notice something important: a rook on an empty board controls fourteen squares no matter where you place it. A rook in the corner on a1 is just as "wide" as a rook in the middle. That makes the rook different from the knight or the bishop, whose power drops sharply at the edge. What limits a rook is not the edge of the board, it is other pieces standing in its way.

## Blocking and capturing

The rook stops at the first piece it meets on a line. If that piece is yours, the rook has to stop before it and cannot move past. If that piece is the enemy's, the rook may capture it by moving onto its square, and then it stops there. It cannot capture a piece and keep sliding, and it cannot capture two pieces on the same line in one move.

Say your rook sits on a1 and the a-file has your own pawn on a2 and a black pawn on a5. The rook is stuck: a2 blocks it immediately, so it has zero moves up the file. Push the pawn to a4 and suddenly nothing changes for the better, because the pawn still blocks. Move the pawn off the file entirely, say a pawn capture like axb5, and the rook now sees a2, a3, a4, a5, a6, a7 and a8, provided those squares are empty. This is why strong players talk about "opening a file for the rook" — the rook does not get stronger, its lines get longer.

Captures are written with an x. A rook on e1 taking a piece on e7 is Rxe7. If two rooks could both reach the same square, you disambiguate with the file or rank you came from: with rooks on a1 and f1 both able to reach d1, you write Rad1 or Rfd1.

## Castling and the seventh rank

The rook is also the only piece other than the king that takes part in castling. On the kingside, 0-0, the white king goes from e1 to g1 and the h1-rook hops over it to f1. On the queenside, 0-0-0, the king goes from e1 to c1 and the a1-rook lands on d1. Castling is the fastest way to take a rook out of the corner and connect it with its partner.

Once your rooks are connected on the back rank and a file opens, look for the enemy's second rank (the seventh rank for White). A rook that reaches the seventh eats pawns that have never moved and pins the enemy king to the back rank. A classic simple pattern: with a white rook on e1 and the e-file cleared of pawns, Re7 attacks the pawns on b7, c7, d7, f7, g7 and h7 all at once from the side, because a rook attacks along the whole rank.

## Rooks and the endgame

In the endgame, when few pieces block the lines, the rook comes into its own. The basic king-and-rook checkmate works because the rook cuts the board in half. Imagine a black king on e5 and a white rook on d1: the rook controls the entire d-file, so the black king can never step to d4, d5 or d6. Bring your own king up, shrink the box one file at a time, and the enemy king is forced to the edge, where a rook check along the back rank ends the game.

That same "cutting" idea explains why a rook belongs behind a passed pawn, whether it is your pawn or the opponent's. Behind your own passer, the rook's line grows as the pawn advances. In front of it, the rook is a blocker doing nothing.

## Common mistakes

- Trying to move a rook diagonally, usually confusing it with the bishop. If you want to remember it, picture the rook as a plus sign and the bishop as an X.
- Forgetting the rook cannot jump. Beginners often "see" Rxa7 while their own pawn still sits on a2. Trace the whole line square by square before you commit.
- Trying to capture two pieces on one line in a single move. The rook stops on the first piece it takes.
- Developing a rook too early by pushing a rook pawn and swinging the rook out, for example 1.a4 and 2.Ra3. The rook has no safe posts in the opening middle and gets chased by minor pieces. Castle first, then open a file, then place the rook.
- Leaving both rooks asleep in the corners for twenty moves. If nothing else is pressing, ask which file is open or about to open, and put a rook there.
