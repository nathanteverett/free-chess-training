## Overview

A pawn moves straight ahead one square. From its starting rank it may instead jump two squares, provided both squares in front of it are empty. It captures differently from how it moves: one square diagonally forward. And it can never, under any circumstance, go backward. That last rule is the one that shapes chess: every pawn move is permanent, so a pawn push is a decision you can never take back.

## The three ways a pawn can move

Start with the quiet push. A white pawn on e2 can step to e3. A black pawn on e7 can step to e6. White pawns always move up the board toward rank 8; black pawns always move down toward rank 1. There is no sideways, no backward, no diagonal unless a capture is involved.

Next, the double step. A pawn that is still on its home rank — rank 2 for White, rank 7 for Black — may push two squares in one move. So 1.e4 is legal because e3 and e4 are both empty. The two-square move is a one-time offer per pawn: once that e-pawn sits on e4, it can only ever go one square at a time, and only forward. Note the requirement carefully. The pawn does not jump over anything. If a knight sits on e3, the pawn on e2 cannot play e4, because the path is blocked. Only knights leap.

Third, the capture. A pawn captures one square diagonally forward, never straight ahead. If a white pawn stands on e4 and a black pawn stands on d5, White can play exd5. But if a black pawn stands directly in front on e5, the white pawn is stuck: it cannot capture forward and it cannot move through. That is why locked pawns like the e4/e5 pair in 1.e4 e5 simply sit there, staring at each other, until one of them is offered a diagonal target.

## Why the double step exists, and what it does to the opening

The two-square rule is not decoration. It exists to speed the game up, and it is the reason the first few moves of almost every game push a central pawn two squares. Play 1.e4 and three things happen at once: the pawn stakes a claim on the d5 and f5 squares, the light-squared bishop on f1 gets a diagonal to c4 or b5, and the queen on d1 can breathe along d1 to h5. The same is true for 1.d4, which frees the c1 bishop and takes control of c5 and e5.

Compare that with 1.e3. Perfectly legal, and not even bad, but you have used a whole move to do half a job. The bishop is freed, but the pawn controls less ground and you may need a second tempo to get it to e4 later.

A concrete opening line shows the pawn rules working together. After 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4, White eyes f7. Now 3...Nf6 4.Ng5 attacks f7 twice, and Black defends with 4...d5, a pawn using its own double step to hit the bishop on c4. White replies 5.exd5, a diagonal pawn capture, and the fight is on. Every one of those pawn moves is a pawn rule in action: e4 and e5 and d5 are double steps, exd5 is a diagonal capture.

## En passant: the double step's price

Because a pawn can leap two squares, it could in theory sneak past an enemy pawn that was waiting to capture it. The en passant rule closes that loophole.

Suppose White has a pawn on e5 and Black plays d7 to d5, sliding past the e5 pawn's diagonal reach. White may capture as if the black pawn had only moved to d6: exd6, removing the pawn from d5 even though the white pawn lands on d6.

<Spoken>En passant works like this. A white pawn on the fifth rank, say e5, and a black pawn that has just played from d7 straight to d5. White captures with e-takes-d6, landing on d6 and removing the black pawn from d5.</Spoken>

Two conditions bind it. Your capturing pawn must be on its fifth rank — rank 5 for White, rank 4 for Black. And you must capture immediately, on the very next move. Play anything else and the right is gone for good.

## Promotion: the reward at the end of the march

A pawn that reaches the far rank — rank 8 for White, rank 1 for Black — must immediately become a queen, rook, bishop, or knight of its own color. It does not stay a pawn, and it does not have the option to stay a pawn. Almost always you take the queen. The exception worth knowing is the underpromotion to a knight, which is right when the knight delivers check or mate that a queen cannot. This is why a single extra pawn in a quiet endgame is worth fighting for: it is a queen in waiting.

Promotion is also the reason "pawns never move backward" cuts both ways. A pawn is your slowest piece and your only piece that improves simply by surviving to the end of its journey.

## Common mistakes

- **Trying to capture straight ahead.** A pawn on e4 cannot take a piece on e5. It captures only to d5 and f5. If an enemy pawn blocks you head-on, you need another piece, or a different pawn, to break the blockade.
- **Assuming the double step can jump a blocker.** With a piece on e3, a pawn on e2 cannot play e4. Both squares must be empty. Only knights jump.
- **Thinking the double step is always available.** It is available only from the pawn's home rank, and only once. A pawn on e3 can never reach e5 in one move.
- **Missing en passant, or trying it a move late.** Players either forget the rule exists and let a pawn slip past, or try to use it two moves after the double step. The capture is legal on the immediately following move only.
- **Pushing pawns for no reason.** Because a pawn move is irreversible, every push permanently weakens the squares that pawn used to guard. Before you push, ask what square you are giving up. Random pawn moves in the opening are how beginners get their kings ripped open.
