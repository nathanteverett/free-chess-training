## Overview

Chess pieces are not equal, and the whole game runs on that fact. Players use a simple scale to compare them: pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9. The king has no number because you can never trade it. These values let you answer the single most common question in a chess game, "if I take that and they take back, did I come out ahead?"

## The six pieces and their numbers

Each side starts with eight pawns, two knights, two bishops, two rooks, one queen and one king. On the back rank from left to right for White, the order is rook a1, knight b1, bishop c1, queen d1, king e1, bishop f1, knight g1, rook h1. The queen always starts on her own color: the white queen on d1, a light square, the black queen on d8, a dark square.

<Spoken>A pawn is worth one point, a knight three, a bishop three, a rook five, and a queen nine. The king is not given a value because losing it ends the game.</Spoken>

The numbers are not magic. They come from how many squares a piece tends to control and how easily it can bring force to a target. A queen on an open board can attack twenty-seven squares. A knight on the edge of the board attacks as few as two or three. That is the whole reason behind the old advice that a knight on the rim is dim.

## Counting a trade

The values only matter when pieces come off. Suppose you play 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5, and Black replies 3...a6 4.Bxc6 dxc6. You gave up a bishop, worth 3, and took a knight, worth 3. That is an even trade. Now suppose instead your opponent leaves a rook on an undefended square and your bishop can take it. Bishop takes rook is plus 5 for you, and even if the rook was defended and they recapture, you have traded 3 points for 5 and gained 2. That difference of 2 is called winning the exchange.

The rule for a capture sequence is simple. Add up what you take, subtract what you lose, and only start the sequence if the total is not negative. If a knight on e5 is defended by a pawn, and you capture it with your rook, you win 3 and lose 5. That is minus 2, a bad trade, even though the knight was "free" for one move.

## Why 3 does not always equal 3

Bishops and knights are both worth about 3, but they are not interchangeable. A bishop only ever touches half the squares on the board. A light-squared bishop can never guard a dark square, no matter how many moves you give it. Two bishops together cover both colors, which is why the bishop pair is often quietly counted as worth a little more than a bishop and a knight, roughly half a pawn.

Bishops love open positions with long clear diagonals. Knights love closed, blocked positions full of pawns, because they hop over the traffic. A knight also has one thing no other piece has: it can attack a queen without the queen being able to attack it back.

Three minor pieces do not equal one queen in any simple way either. Nine points of pawns is not nine points of queen. A queen coordinates alone; nine pawns have to be defended, pushed, and shepherded. Treat the numbers as a first estimate, then look at the actual position.

## Pawns and the eighth rank

A pawn is worth 1, which makes it sound disposable. But a pawn that reaches the far end of the board promotes, almost always to a queen. That single-point piece turns into nine points. This is why endgames with almost no material are still sharp, and why you should never shrug off a pawn loss with "it was only a pawn."

Pawns also gain and lose value by position. A passed pawn on the seventh rank, with nothing in its way, can be worth more than a knight, because it forces the opponent to tie a piece down to stop it. A doubled, isolated pawn on the a-file is often worth much less than 1.

## When the numbers lie

Material is one factor among several. King safety can overrule everything. If your opponent's king is exposed and your pieces are pointed at it, being down a rook can be irrelevant, because checkmate ends the game regardless of the score sheet. Legall's Mate is the classic beginner example: after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.Bc4 Bg4 4.Nc3 g6, White plays 5.Nxe5, ignoring the fact that Black's bishop on g4 attacks the queen. If Black grabs it with 5...Bxd1, White mates with 6.Bxf7+ Ke7 7.Nd5#. White gave up nine points and won the game.

Activity matters too. A rook stuck on h1 behind its own pieces is not doing 5 points of work. Count the material, then ask which side's pieces are actually doing something.

## Common mistakes

- Taking a defended piece just because you can. Always check what recaptures. A "free" knight guarded by a pawn costs you 5 to win 3 if you take with a rook.
- Trading a bishop for a knight for no reason, especially early. The trade is even on points, but you give up half of the bishop pair and often the better piece in an open position.
- Treating a pawn as worthless. Pawns promote. Losing one pawn in a quiet position is often enough to lose the endgame.
- Grabbing material while your king is still in the center. Two extra pawns will not save an uncastled king from a coordinated attack.
- Counting only the pieces you capture and forgetting the recapture chain. Count both sides of the exchange all the way to the end before you commit.
